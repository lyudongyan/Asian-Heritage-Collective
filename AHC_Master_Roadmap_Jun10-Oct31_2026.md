## Part 0 — The honest framing (read this first)

Two things are true at once, and the whole plan depends on holding both:

**1. "Founder of a nonprofit" is one of the most common archetypes in T10 applicant pools.** Admissions readers at Harvard see hundreds of student-founded 501(c)(3)s per cycle, and most are thin: a website, a board of friends, a few hundred dollars raised, founded junior year, abandoned after acceptance. Readers are calibrated to discount the *title* and look for evidence the organization is real.

**2. AHC is verifiably real, and that's your entire advantage.** A 2,500-person Eastern Market audience, 3,600+ certified service hours, a Candid Silver Seal, nine institutional partners who invite you back, an EIN, a functioning site, and a 14-person team are things almost no student nonprofit has. The job between now and October 31 is not to make AHC *look* impressive — it's to make the existing reality **externally verifiable** (press, awards, certifications, published numbers) and **deeper** (sustained programs, succession, scale), because verification and depth are exactly what separates a real organization from a resume nonprofit in a reader's eyes.

Everything below serves one of four admissions-relevant qualities, in priority order:

1. **Verifiable impact** — numbers a reader can check (press clips, Candid profile, impact report, PVSA).
2. **Depth and duration** — founded Oct 2023, three years of sustained, growing programming by application time. Duration is rarer than scale.
3. **A coherent personal narrative** — AHC isn't a generic service club; it's the institutional answer to a personal question (*"why did I quit Chinese school, and what would have made me stay?"*). That story is yours alone and no other applicant can copy it.
4. **Leadership that outlives you** — succession planning is the single most underused signal. An org designed to survive its founder reads as institution-building; an org that dies at graduation reads as an application prop.

One more honest note: nothing guarantees admission anywhere, and Harvard REA admits ~3–4% of an extremely strong pool. The right mental model is that AHC, done well, makes you a *competitive* applicant with a distinctive spike — and is independently worth doing. Build it like it matters beyond the application, because that authenticity is also what reads best.

## Part 1 — The unifying narrative (decide this week, before anything else)

Every initiative, essay, and press pitch should ladder up to **one sentence**:

> *"I watched my generation quit their heritage because it was taught as an obligation — so I built an organization that teaches it as something you do: perform it for 2,500 people, teach it to a kid, melt it into a crayon."*

This sentence does three jobs: it explains the founding (personal), it explains the method (participatory, hands-on — your stated thesis), and it makes every program legible as one idea rather than a grab bag. When choosing between two opportunities this summer, pick the one that fits this sentence.

**What this means concretely:**

- The tutoring program is not "tutoring" — it's *"the Chinese school I wish I'd had."* Your blog post title already is the pitch.
- The crayon program is not "recycling" — it's *"culture as something you make with your hands,"* feeding free kits to libraries and Head Start kids.
- TEDx is not "an event" — it's *"giving heritage stories a stage."*
- Chapters are not "expansion" — they're *"proving the model works without me."*

## Part 5 — The admissions layer

### 5.1 How readers will actually evaluate this

Harvard's process rates extracurriculars on a numeric scale; the top ratings are reserved for *national-level distinction or truly unusual impact*. A regional org with press coverage, institutional partners, certified hours in the thousands, and national recognition (Candid, PVSA, award shortlists) plausibly reads at the strong end. What moves the rating: **external validation, scale relative to age, duration, and whether *you* clearly drove it.** What doesn't: titles, logo polish, follower counts, or breadth of programs nobody can verify.

Your application's job in one line: make a skeptical 8-minute reader conclude *"this one is real."*

### 5.2 Common App mechanics (start in August, finalize early October)

- **Activities list (10 slots, 150 chars each).** AHC should occupy **one slot** (Founder/Executive Director), written dense with verifiable numbers. Draft to refine:
  > *Founded 501(c)(3) cultural nonprofit: 14-student team, 8,000+ reached, 4,500 certified service hrs, 16 events incl. performance for 2,500; Candid Silver Seal.*
  Resist spreading AHC across multiple slots (e.g., separate "tutoring program" entry) unless you genuinely lack other activities — it reads as padding. Exception: if TEDx happens, a separate "TEDx organizer (licensed)" entry is legitimate since it's an externally licensed role.
- **Honors section (5 slots):** Candid Silver Seal, PVSA (your own certified hours — as a certifying org, certify your own award), any award wins/shortlists from 5.4, press features if slots remain.
- **Additional Information:** 2–4 restrained sentences + link to the site/impact report. Not an essay — an index. E.g., *"AHC's audited impact data, annual report, and press coverage: asianheritagecollective.org/impact."* Consider creating that `/impact` page in October.
- **Harvard supplements:** at least one short answer should carry the AHC story from the *inside* — a specific moment (a kid at a library table, a tutoring student's score, the Eastern Market crowd going quiet) rather than the org's stats, which live elsewhere.

### 5.3 The essay (start drafting in June, not September)

The strongest available angle is the one you already wrote: **"I Quit Chinese School. Then I Got a 5 on the AP Exam."** The personal statement should probably be the *prequel* to AHC — the quitting, the complicated identity of a second-generation kid, the realization that heritage taught as obligation fails — with AHC appearing late as the consequence, not the subject. Essays *about* one's nonprofit tend to read as extended resumes; essays about the question the nonprofit answers read as a person. Draft in June–July while it's low-stakes; you'll need August–September for supplements.

### 5.5 Recommendations and interview

- **Optional/supplemental recommendation:** an adult from a partner institution who has watched AHC over time — Mrs. Lien (MCWA) is ideal: she's hosted you twice, her testimonial is already on your site, and a community leader vouching that a student org delivered real value to her 400-person banquet is exactly the third-party verification readers want. Ask in September, after the banquet.
- **Counselor:** brief them with a one-page AHC summary in early fall (they write from what they know).
- **Interview prep (Oct–Dec):** your contemporaneous activity log becomes your story bank. Prepare the failure stories too — what didn't work, what you'd redo — interviewers probe founders hardest on whether they actually ran the thing.

### 5.6 Succession (do this even though it's after Oct 31 in effect)

Name and begin grooming a successor (or co-directors) by early fall, and *say so* — in the impact report ("leadership transition plan"), in interviews, in essays if natural. The chapter toolkit doubles as the succession manual. An org that announces its plan to outlive its founder preempts the #1 skepticism about student nonprofits. It's also simply the right thing to do for the 13 other people on the team.

## Part 6 — What *not* to do (scope discipline)

- **Don't add a seventh initiative.** The portfolio is full. Anything new before Nov 1 should replace something, not stack.
- **Don't let TEDx eat September.** Decision gate mid-July, separate lead, minimal scope or public reschedule.
- **Don't claim partnerships that don't exist** (Crayon Collection, "international chapters" that are one Discord call). Readers and journalists both check; one inflated claim taints every real number.
- **Don't optimize visibly for admissions.** No "as featured in my Harvard application" energy anywhere public. The org's public story is the mission; the application's story is you. Keep them separate and both stay credible.
- **Don't neglect schoolwork and the application itself.** A transcript dip or rushed essays costs more than two extra events gain. The founder's calendar from August on must reserve essay time as a fixed block, not a leftover.
- **Don't burn out the team.** Eleven students with summer jobs are running this. The delegation map exists; use it. Your three jobs remain: partners, press, weekly 30-minute check-ins — plus, now, your own application.
